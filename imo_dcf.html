4. COST OF EQUITY CALCULATION
▶

4.1 Cost of Equity (CAPM)

Cost of Equity = Rf + β × (Rm - Rf) + Country Risk Premium

Where:
• Rf = 3.3% (Government of Canada 10-year — a C$ discount rate for C$ cash flows)
• β = 1.214 (Levered Beta from Section 1)
• Rm - Rf = 5.00% (Market Risk Premium from Section 2)
• Country Risk Premium = 0.38% (from Section 3)

Calculation:
Cost of Equity = 3.3% + (1.038 × 5.00%) + 0.29%
= 3.3% + 5.190% + 0.29%
= 8.780%

Why a Canadian risk-free rate. The free cash flows in Section 7 are Canadian dollars, and a discount rate has to be denominated in the same currency as the cash flows it discounts — otherwise the expected inflation differential between the two currencies is silently dropped. So the CAPM here uses the Government of Canada 10-year, while the S&P dividend model in Section 2 is differenced against the US 10-year, which is what it is actually built from. The two risk-free rates are separate inputs under EDIT MODEL. The conversion to US dollars happens once, at the end, in Section 9.3.

7.1 Normalized Base Free Cash Flow (H1 2026 Annualized)

Line Item H1 2026 (C$M) Annualized (C$M) Source

Reconciliation to the filing's own cash flow statement — and why they differ.
This table builds free cash flow the standard way: tax the operating result, add back non-cash D&A, subtract capital spending. Imperial's cash flow statement tells a somewhat different story for the same six months, and the gap is worth understanding before relying on the number above.

Cash from operating activities was C$3,460M for the half; capital and exploration expenditures were C$1,009M. Annualised, that is roughly C$6,920M less C$2,010M, or about C$4,910M of actual free cash flow — against the C$6,253M this model uses. The difference comes from three places, all visible in the filing: cash taxes ran ahead of book taxes by C$386M in the half (the deferred income tax line), working capital absorbed roughly C$259M, and pension contributions took another C$74M.

Those are timing items rather than permanent leakage, which is the usual argument for ignoring them in a going-concern DCF — and it is why the model leaves the change in net working capital at zero. But they have run in the same direction here, so treat the figure above as the optimistic end. Setting the change in net working capital to about 260 per period under EDIT MODEL moves the base free cash flow to the cash statement's neighbourhood, and the valuation with it.

One item the parser cannot see. Note 11 records a C$50M release of the 2025 restructuring provision, credited to Selling and general — so it lifts the reported pre-tax income above. It is tagged only inside a dimensional context, which this page's reader deliberately skips, so the restructuring line above reads zero. To remove it, enter −50 in that field under EDIT MODEL. It is worth about 0.6% of the valuation.

7.2 FCF Projection Assumptions (5-Year Forecast)

READ THIS BEFORE TRUSTING THE NUMBER: the base period is a strong one.
Imperial is a commodity producer, and this model annualises a half-year in which crude and refining margins were favourable. H1 2026 pre-tax income was C$4,092M against C$2,932M in H1 2025 — up 40%. The second quarter alone was C$2,860M against C$1,244M — up 130%. Doubling that half-year, as the model does, assumes the whole of the next five years looks like the best six months in recent memory.

This is the single largest source of error in the valuation, and it is much larger than any beta or discount-rate debate. At the H1 2025 run rate the base free cash flow — and with it the whole answer — is roughly 30% lower. Two honest ways to handle it: drop in the FY 10-K instead of this 10-Q so the base spans a full cycle year, or edit income before income taxes down by hand to a mid-cycle figure under EDIT MODEL.
Years 1-3:
- Production is capacity-constrained: Imperial's oil sands assets grow volumes in the low single digits, not by step change
- Capital spending of C$1,009M for the half against D&A of C$1,031M — capex is running at maintenance level, which supports flat-to-slow real growth and no more
- Crude prices are assumed to neither rise nor fall in real terms, since forecasting them is not something this model is equipped to do
- Assumed FCF Growth Rate: 2% per year — essentially inflation, no volume story

Years 4-5:
- Same reasoning; nothing in the filing supports an inflection either way
- Assumed FCF Growth Rate: 2% per year

Terminal Growth Rate: 2.1% (long-term GDP growth)
Rationale: a mature producer in a mature economy should not compound faster than the economy forever. Note that for a company facing eventual decline in its end market, even 2.1% in perpetuity is an optimistic assumption rather than a conservative one.
